The lecture describes the brainstem, the stalk connecting the cerebrum to the spinal cord and cerebellum, made of the midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata, with its embryological origin, connections and external and internal configuration. It dwells on where each cranial nerve emerges, on the fourth ventricle, the reticular formation and the running tracts, and on the fact that the brainstem runs vital functions. It ends with the blood supply from the basilar artery and the alternating syndrome that locates a lesion to a level of the brainstem.

## Worker's notes

- The packet contains no known-concept list, so all concept names are my own standard English forms.
- The concept count (71) is above the typical range because the lecture names ten cranial nerves, each with its own emergence landmark, plus many surface and ventricular landmarks that are tested individually.
- Cranial nerve emergence has no dedicated relation type. I used branch-of (read as 'arises from') for a nerve emerging from a part of the brainstem, located-in for a nerve emerging at a named landmark (interpeduncular fossa, pontomedullary junction, cerebellopontine angle), and related-to for emergence in front of or behind the olive or pyramid.
- 'Bulbopontine groove' (summary table and slide 20) is given as an alias of 'Pontomedullary junction' because the packet lists the same nerves (VI, VII, VIII, medial to lateral) at both; the packet never states the equivalence outright.
- hy:1 writes 'Midbrain (mesencephalon, smallest)' as if mesencephalon were a synonym of midbrain; I kept Mesencephalon, Metencephalon and Myelencephalon as separate concepts for the embryonic vesicles, as the embryology table and questions treat them.

- Slide 20 uses older nerve names from OCR text: 'common occulomotor nerve' (III), 'external oculomotor' (VI), 'pathetic nerve' (IV), 'cochleo-vestibular' (VIII) and 'the spinal nerve' (XI); slide 17 instead speaks of the 'cranial part of accessory'. These are recorded as aliases.
