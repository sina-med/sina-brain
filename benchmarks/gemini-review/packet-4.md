[sec:anat3-brainstem/big] The big picture
The brainstem is the stalk connecting the cerebrum to the spinal cord, with the cerebellum hanging behind it. Three parts from top to bottom: midbrain, pons, medulla.
Two things carry the lecture: where each cranial nerve emerges, and the fact that the brainstem runs the vital functions, breathing, heart rate, blood pressure and consciousness.

[sec:anat3-brainstem/intro] Introduction and embryology
The brainstem connects the cerebrum to the spinal cord and cerebellum. It lies in the posterior cranial fossa, on the clivus, under the tentorium cerebelli: above the cord, below the cerebrum and in front of the cerebellum. Ten of the twelve cranial nerves arise from its nuclei.
Part | Embryonic vesicle
Medulla oblongata | Myelencephalon (fifth vesicle)
Pons | Metencephalon (fourth vesicle)
Midbrain | Mesencephalon (third vesicle)
The growing neural tube bends at the cephalic flexure and the mesencephalic (cranial) flexure.
Connections. Above, the diencephalon by the transverse fissure of Bichat. Behind, the cerebellum by the cerebellar peduncles: superior to the midbrain, middle to the pons, inferior to the medulla. Below, the spinal cord through the foramen magnum.

[sec:anat3-brainstem/external] External configuration
Midbrain
The smallest part, about 2 cm, passing through the tentorial opening (foramen of Pacchioni). Two parts:
- Tectum, behind the cerebral aqueduct: two superior and two inferior colliculi below the pineal gland. The superior brachium links the superior colliculus with the retina (vision); the inferior brachium carries lateral lemniscus and inferior colliculus fibres to the medial geniculate body (hearing). CN IV emerges below the colliculi.
- Cerebral peduncles, paired and anterolateral, separated by the interpeduncular fossa. CN III exits between them; the optic tract runs round the top of the midbrain.
Pons
The largest part, from the metencephalon with the cerebellum. Its ventral bulge, about 2.5 cm, is made by transverse pontocerebellar fibres. The basilar groove holds the basilar artery. The pontomedullary junction is a key landmark.
Nerve | Emerges at
CN V, trigeminal | Lateral mid-pons
CN VI, abducens | Pontomedullary junction, near the midline
CN VII, facial | Cerebellopontine angle
CN VIII, vestibulocochlear | Lateral to CN VII
Posterior surface, the floor of the fourth ventricle: the medial eminence in the midline; the facial colliculus, facial fibres looping round the abducens nucleus; and the stria medullaris marking the pons-medulla border. The cerebellopontine angle, where CN VII and VIII emerge beside the foramen of Luschka, lies at the junction of pons, medulla and cerebellum.
Medulla
The ventral median fissure, continuous with the cord's, is marked below by the decussation of 75 to 90 percent of corticospinal fibres. The pyramid on each side is made by the corticospinal tract; the olive lateral to it by the inferior olivary nucleus. CN XII emerges between pyramid and olive; CN IX, X and XI behind the olive.

[sec:anat3-brainstem/nerves] Emergence of the cranial nerves
Surface | Nerves
Anterior, midbrain | III between the cerebral peduncles
Anterior, pons | V
Anterior, bulbopontine groove | VI, VII, VII bis (intermediate nerve of Wrisberg) and VIII, from medial to lateral
Anterior, opposite the olive | XII
Lateral, behind the olive | IX, X and XI, the mixed nerves
Posterior, midbrain | IV, the only nerve emerging dorsally
Two rules. All cranial nerves arise from the brainstem except the optic and olfactory nerves. All emerge from the anterolateral surface except the trochlear nerve.

[sec:anat3-brainstem/internal] Internal configuration
As in the cord: white matter (descending motor and ascending sensory pathways), an ependymal cavity (the fourth ventricle and aqueduct of Sylvius) and grey matter (the cranial nerve nuclei and the reticular formation).
Reticular formation. Cell bodies and small axon bundles running in many directions, without distinct boundaries, spreading from the cord through the brainstem to the hypothalamus and thalamus. It integrates vital functions: arousal, consciousness, sleep-wake cycles, motor coordination, cardiovascular and respiratory control, pain modulation and habituation.
Tract | Type | Carries
Corticospinal and corticobulbar | Descending | Voluntary movement of body and face
Dorsal column-medial lemniscus | Ascending, conscious | Touch, proprioception, vibration
Anterolateral (spinothalamic) | Ascending, conscious | Crude touch, pain, temperature
Spinocerebellar | Ascending, unconscious | Proprioception to the cerebellum
Fourth ventricle. Roof: the superior and inferior medullary vela. Floor: the rhomboid fossa. Side walls: the vela and cerebellar peduncles. The peak is the fastigium; the caudal tip the obex; widest at the pontomedullary junction. Choroid plexus lines the roof, and CSF leaves by the median foramen of Magendie and two lateral foramina of Luschka.

[sec:anat3-brainstem/vessels] Blood supply, functions and clinical aspects
Blood supply: the basilar artery and its branches: the posterior cerebral artery and its peduncular branch, the superior cerebellar artery, the posterior choroidal artery and the interpeduncular branches.
Functions: a pathway for tracts between cortex and cord; the site of origin and emergence of CN III to XII; and the reticular formation, controlling consciousness, pain perception and cardiovascular and respiratory regulation.
Alternating syndrome. A peripheral cranial nerve lesion on one side with a hemiplegia on the other locates the lesion to a level in the brainstem.
Your professor's review questions: the thalamus is not part of the brainstem; the pyramids decussate in the medulla; the aqueduct passes through the midbrain; CN IV emerges dorsally; the respiratory and cardiovascular centres are in the medulla.

[deeper:1] Why an alternating syndrome points to one level
A cranial nerve leaves the brainstem on its own side, while the corticospinal fibres running past it have not yet crossed, doing so in the lower medulla. A single small lesion therefore hits the cranial nerve on one side and the motor tract heading for the opposite limbs. The cranial nerve involved tells you the level: CN III for the midbrain, CN VI or VII for the pons, CN XII for the medulla.
Source: the lecture's alternating syndrome slide, read with its cranial nerve emergence table.

[deeper:2] Why brainstem lesions are so dangerous
Everything is packed into a few centimetres: every tract between brain and body, the nuclei of ten cranial nerves, and the reticular formation keeping you awake and breathing. A small stroke or a raised pressure pushing the brainstem down through the foramen magnum can stop breathing or consciousness outright, which is why the brainstem is called a vital centre.
Source: the lecture's functions slide and its take-home messages.

[deeper:3] Why CN IV is so easily injured
The trochlear nerve is the only one emerging from the back of the brainstem, the thinnest cranial nerve, and it has the longest course inside the skull as it wraps around the midbrain. That long exposed path is why it is often damaged in head injury, causing vertical double vision that the patient compensates for by tilting the head.
Source: the lecture's note that CN IV is the only nerve emerging dorsally.

[deeper:4] Why a tumour at the cerebellopontine angle causes deafness first
CN VII and VIII emerge side by side at the cerebellopontine angle. A tumour of the vestibulocochlear nerve sheath grows there and presses on CN VIII first, causing one-sided hearing loss and tinnitus, then later on CN VII and even CN V. The lecture's description of the angle is exactly why these tumours present the way they do.

The Reticular Formation
• The reticular formation (RF) consists of an
intricate mixture of neuronal cell bodies and
fascicles of axons running in small bundles,
which are oriented in many different directions.
• It does not have any distinct cytoarchitectural
boundaries and is dispersed throughout the
brainstem
• It is like a network extending from the spinal cord
through the medulla, the pons, the midbrain, the
subthalamus, hypothalamus and the thalamus
• serve as a major integration and relay center for
many vital brain systems to coordinate functions
necessary for survival: arousal, consciousness,
circadian rhythm (sleep-wake cycles),
coordination of somatic motor movements,
cardiovascular and respiratory control, pain
modulation, and habituation.

[slide:anat3-brainstem/24] (slide 24)
The Running Tracts
• Several ascending and descending tracts travel the entire brainstem.
• The most important descending one is the motor pathway, which is
composed by the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts. It controls
the voluntary skeletal muscle activity of the body and face.
• There are several important ascending tracts that transport all the
sensations from the periphery to the cerebral cortex. Functionally, the
ascending tracts can be divided into the type of information they
transmit:
➢Conscious tracts : comprised of the dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathway
and the anterolateral system.
➢Unconscious tracts : comprised of the spinocerebellar tracts.

FOURTH VENTRICLE
• The roof, located dorsally is formed by the superior
medullary velum and inferior medullary velum overlying
the cerebellum.
• The floor, located ventrally, is formed by the rhomboid
fossa, a diamond-shaped depression on the dorsal surface of
the pons and upper half of the medulla.

• The fourth ventricle contains choroid plexus along its roof
along the tela choroidea which may protrude out the median
foramina of Magendi and 2 lateral foraminas of Luschka.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b1]
Front: What is the brainstem, and what does it do?
Back: It connects the cerebrum to the spinal cord and cerebellum. Three parts, descending: midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata. Ten of the twelve cranial nerves arise from its nuclei. It controls vital functions: breathing, consciousness, blood pressure, heart rate and sleep.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b2]
Front: Where does the brainstem lie?
Back: In the posterior cranial fossa, on the clivus of the occipital bone, under the tentorium cerebelli: above the spinal cord, below the cerebrum and in front of the cerebellum.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b3]
Front: Give the embryological origin of each part.
Back: Medulla from the myelencephalon (fifth vesicle), pons from the metencephalon (fourth vesicle), midbrain from the mesencephalon (third vesicle). The growing neural tube bends at the cephalic and mesencephalic flexures.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s1]
Front: Which part of the brainstem is at the arrow?
Back: The midbrain, the smallest part, about 2 cm, containing the tectum and cerebral peduncles.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b4]

Back: Above: the diencephalon, by the transverse fissure of Bichat. Behind: the cerebellum, by the cerebellar peduncles. Below: the spinal cord, through the foramen magnum.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b5]
Front: Which cerebellar peduncle goes where?
Back: Superior: midbrain. Middle: pons. Inferior: medulla oblongata.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b6]
Front: Describe the midbrain.
Back: The smallest part, about 2 cm, passing through the tentorial opening, the foramen of Pacchioni. Two parts: the tectum behind the cerebral aqueduct and the paired cerebral peduncles in front.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s2]

Back: The pons, the largest part, its bulge made by transverse pontocerebellar fibres.

Back: Four colliculi, two superior and two inferior, just below the pineal gland. Back: Formed by the back of the pons and medulla. The medial eminence marks the midline. The facial colliculus is a bulge of facial nerve fibres looping round the abducens nucleus. The stria medullaris crosses transversely and marks the border between pons and medulla.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b12]

Back: The angle at the junction of pons, medulla and cerebellum, where the flocculus, the choroid plexus and the emerging CN VII and VIII surround the lateral apertures of the fourth ventricle, the foramina of Luschka.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s4]
Front: Which elevation is at the arrow, and what makes it?
Back: The pyramid, made by the corticospinal tract, whose fibres mostly cross in the lower medulla.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b13]
Front: Describe the ventral surface of the medulla.
Back: The ventral median fissure, continuous with the cord's, whose lower part is marked by the decussation of 75 to 90 percent of corticospinal fibres. The pyramid on each side, made by the corticospinal tract. The olive lateral to it, made by the inferior olivary nucleus, important in movement control.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b14]
Front: Which nerves emerge from the medulla?
Back: Four: CN XII from the sulcus between pyramid and olive; CN IX, X and the cranial part of XI, from above downwards, from the sulcus behind the olive.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b15]
Front: Give the general internal organisation of the brainstem.
Back: As in the cord: white matter with descending motor and ascending sensory pathways; an ependymal cavity, the fourth ventricle and aqueduct of Sylvius; and grey matter, the cranial nerve nuclei and the reticular formation.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s5]
Front: Which elevation is at the arrow?
Back: The olive, lateral to the pyramid, made by the inferior olivary nucleus.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b16]
Front: Summarise the emergence of the cranial nerves.
Back: Anterior surface: III between the peduncles; V from the pons; VI, VII, VII bis and VIII at the bulbopontine groove, from medial to lateral; XII opposite the olive. Lateral surface, behind the olive: IX, X and XI. Posterior surface: IV, the only nerve emerging dorsally.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b17]
Front: Two rules about the cranial nerves.
Back: All arise from the brainstem except the optic and olfactory nerves. All emerge from the anterolateral surface except the trochlear nerve, which emerges dorsally from the midbrain.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b18]
Front: What is the reticular formation?
Back: A mixture of neuronal cell bodies and small bundles of axons running in many directions, without distinct boundaries, spread through the brainstem from the cord to the thalamus. It integrates vital systems: arousal, consciousness, sleep-wake cycles, motor coordination, cardiovascular and respiratory control, pain modulation and habituation.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s6]
Front: Which cranial nerve emerges at the arrow?
Back: The trigeminal nerve (CN V), from the lateral aspect of the mid-pons.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b19]
Front: Name the tracts running through the brainstem.
Back: Descending: the motor pathway, corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, for voluntary movement of body and face. Ascending conscious: the dorsal column-medial lemniscus (touch, proprioception, vibration) and the anterolateral or spinothalamic system (crude touch, pain, temperature). [card:anat3-brainstem/b20]
Front: Describe the fourth ventricle.
Back: Roof: the superior and inferior medullary vela over the cerebellum. Floor: the rhomboid fossa. Side walls: the vela and cerebellar peduncles. Its peak in the cerebellum is the fastigium; its caudal tip the obex; widest at the pontomedullary junction. Choroid plexus lines the roof, and CSF leaves by the median foramen of Magendie and two lateral foramina of Luschka.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b21]
Front: Describe the blood supply of the brainstem.
Back: The basilar artery and its branches: the posterior cerebral artery and its peduncular branch, the superior cerebellar artery, the posterior choroidal artery and the interpeduncular branches.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s7]
Front: Which cranial nerve emerges at the arrow?
Back: The oculomotor nerve (CN III), between the cerebral peduncles.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b22]
Front: List the functions of the brainstem.
Back: A pathway for tracts between cortex and cord; the site of origin of the nuclei of CN III to XII; the site of emergence of CN III to XII; and the reticular formation, controlling consciousness, pain perception and cardiovascular and respiratory regulation.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b23]
Front: What is an alternating syndrome?
Back: A peripheral cranial nerve lesion associated with a hemiplegia on the opposite side. It locates the level of the lesion in the brainstem.

[card:anat3-brainstem/b24]
Front: Where are the respiratory and cardiovascular centres?
Back: In the medulla oblongata, which is why a lesion there is life-threatening.

[card:anat3-brainstem/s8]
Front: Which cranial nerve emerges at the arrow?
Back: The hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), from the sulcus between pyramid and olive.
