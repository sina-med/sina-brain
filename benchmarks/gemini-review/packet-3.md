[sec:anat3-larynx/big] The big picture
The larynx is a box of nine cartilages held together by joints and ligaments, with muscles that swing the arytenoids to open and close the vocal cords. Everything in this lecture hangs on two questions: which muscle moves the cords which way, and which nerve supplies it.
The two rules that carry most of the exam: the posterior cricoarytenoid is the only abductor, and the cricothyroid is the only muscle not supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

[sec:anat3-larynx/intro] Introduction and situation
The larynx is the upper expanded part of the lower respiratory tract, modified for producing voice, also called the organ of phonation. It is an unpaired median tube of fibro-muscular-cartilaginous constitution: several cartilages linked by joints, muscles and ligaments, lined on their deep surface by a mucous membrane.
It acts as a sphincter at the inlet of the lower respiratory tract, protecting the trachea and bronchial tree from anything other than air. In laryngeal incompetence, food and fluid may be aspirated. Its upward and downward movements also help in swallowing.
Functions: a) phonation, b) respiration, c) protection, d) deglutition.
Aspect | Detail
Situation | The middle part of the visceral sheath of the neck. Above: the hyoid bone and base of the tongue. Below: the trachea. Behind: the hypopharynx. In front: the thyroid
Age | Higher in children (C2 to C3) than in adults. In newborns it contacts the veil, which is why they keep exclusive nasal breathing until about four months
Extension | Superior: the upper border of the thyroid cartilage, at the body of C4. Inferior: the lower edge of the cricoid cartilage, at the lower edge of C6
Shape and size | A triangular pyramid with a posterosuperior base (hyoid bone, pharynx) and a lower vertex (trachea). In men: length 4.5 cm, transverse diameter 4 cm, sagittal diameter 3.5 cm. Smaller in women
Fixation | Mobile vertically and transversely, but fixed by its continuity with the pharynx and trachea, and by its union with the hyoid bone and thoracic skeleton through muscles and ligaments

[sec:anat3-larynx/cartilages] Cartilages
Nine cartilages. Three unpaired and large: thyroid, cricoid, epiglottis. Three paired and small: arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform. The principal ones are the cricoid, the thyroid and the two arytenoids.
Cartilage | Description
Thyroid | Shaped like an open book, open backwards, with two vertical quadrilateral laminae. Each has an anterolateral side, a posteromedial side (smooth, related to the piriform sinus), a superior border with the superior thyroid incisure, an inferior border with the inferior thyroid incisure, and a thick vertical posterior margin extended by the superior and inferior cornua, the inferior carrying a cricoid articular surface
Epiglottis | Shaped like a tennis racket with a curved lower handle forming its foot. Laryngeal (posterior) side: respiratory mucosa with cribriform depressions, facing the vestibule. Lingual (anterior) side: concave, facing the base of the tongue and hyoid. Base superior and free; vertex or foot inferior, inserting into the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage through the thyroepiglottic ligament
Cricoid | A signet-shaped ring with a narrow anterior arch and a broad posterior lamina, at C6. The only cartilage that completely encircles the lumen of the larynx, and the foundation stone of the larynx. The posterior lamina has a median ridge with a depressed area on each side
Arytenoid | Triangular pyramids about 15 mm high. The apex articulates with the corniculate cartilage, the concave base with the cricoid lamina. The anterior angle of the base extends as the vocal process (insertion of the vocal ligament); the lateral angle bears the muscular process (insertion of the posterior and lateral cricoarytenoid muscles); the posterior side takes the transverse arytenoid muscle
Accessory | Corniculate: on the apices of the arytenoids. Cuneiform: in the aryepiglottic fold. Others: posterior and anterior sesamoids and interarytenoid

[sec:anat3-larynx/joints] Joints and ligaments
Joint | Detail
Cricoarytenoid | The most important functionally. An elliptical synovial articulation reinforced by the posterior cricoarytenoid ligament, uniting the base of the arytenoid with the upper edge of the cricoid lamina. 1. Rotatory movement around a vertical axis, abducting or adducting the vocal cords. 2. Gliding movement, one arytenoid toward or away from the other, closing or opening the posterior part of the glottis
Cricothyroid | A flat synovial joint reinforced by ligaments, joining the lower cornu of the thyroid cartilage and the lateral face of the cricoid. Tilting of the cricoid around a transverse axis (arch up, lamina down) tenses the vocal cords
Extrinsic ligaments and membranes attach the cartilages to neighbouring structures and lie outside the inner fibroelastic tube:
- Thyrohyoid membrane: from the upper edge of the thyroid cartilage to the lower edge of the hyoid bone, with a medial thickening (medial thyroid ligament) and two lateral thickenings (lateral thyroid ligaments).
- Thyroepiglottic ligament: attaches the lower narrow end of the epiglottis to the posterior surface of the thyroid angle.
- Hyoepiglottic ligament: connects the posterior aspect of the hyoid with the anterior surface of the upper end of the epiglottis.
- Cricotracheal ligament: between the lower edge of the cricoid and the upper edge of the first tracheal ring.
Intrinsic ligaments are parts of a broad sheet of fibroelastic tissue forming the inner tube of the laryngeal cavity outside its mucous lining. The cricothyroid ligament, stretched between the thyroid cartilage and the vocal process, forms the vocal ligament. The vestibular ligament is tense between the thyroid angle and the anterolateral aspect of the arytenoid.

[sec:anat3-larynx/muscles] Muscles
Intrinsic muscles, by action
- Vocal cord tensors: cricothyroid (paired). Action: elevation of the cricoid arch and lowering of the thyroid cartilage, tensing the vocal cord.
- Dilator of the glottis: the posterior cricoarytenoid, the only one.
- Constrictors of the glottis: lateral cricoarytenoid, interarytenoid, superior thyroarytenoid (from the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage to the lower third of the arytenoid) and inferior thyroarytenoid or vocal muscle (from the reentrant angle to the anterolateral aspect of the arytenoid), which tilts the arytenoid forward and tenses the cord.
Function | Muscles
Abduct the vocal cords | Posterior cricoarytenoids
Adduct the vocal cords | Lateral cricoarytenoids, transverse arytenoid
Tense the vocal cords | Cricothyroid, vocalis
Relax the vocal cords | Thyroarytenoid
Three exceptions worth memorising. The cricothyroid is the only muscle lying on the outer aspect of the larynx. All the intrinsic muscles are paired except the transverse arytenoid (interarytenoid), which is unpaired. All of them adduct the vocal cords except the posterior cricoarytenoids, which abduct them.
Extrinsic muscles ensure laryngeal mobility, especially during swallowing. Elevators: thyrohyoid, mylohyoid, digastric. Depressors: sternothyroid, sternohyoid, omohyoid.

[sec:anat3-larynx/topo] Topographical anatomy and histology
Compartment | Contents
Supraglottic | Vestibule: a pyramidal space from the laryngeal margin to the ventricular bands. Laryngeal or Morgagni ventricle: between the ventricular bands (false vocal cords) above and the plane of the vocal cords below. Interarytenoid region: the posterior part, where the mucosa covers the fibres of the interarytenoid muscle
Glottis | Two pearly white vocal cords stretched between the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage in front and the arytenoid behind, made of vocal ligament and muscle. Anteriorly they unite at the anterior commissure, attached directly to the thyroid cartilage and containing vessels and lymphatics. Posteriorly they are separated by the interarytenoid region, the posterior commissure. The glottis is triangular and narrower than the vestibular cleft: a narrow zone in children, where obstructive oedema causes respiratory distress very rapidly
Infraglottic | The cavity below the vocal cords, surrounded by the cricoid and the cricothyroid ligament
Histology. The laryngeal mucosa is thin, smooth and pale pink, continuous above with the pharyngeal mucosa and below with the tracheal mucosa. The epithelium is respiratory type (cylindric stratified) throughout, except at the vocal cords and the upper half of the epiglottis, which are non-keratinized stratified squamous.
Relations. Above: the base of the tongue. Below: the trachea. In front: the musculoaponeurotic planes of the subhyoid region and the covering planes, where the larynx is very superficial. Behind: the hypopharynx and cervical spine. Laterally: the piriform sinuses medial to the thyroid cartilage, then the thyroid lobes, and further out the elements of the carotid region.

[sec:anat3-larynx/vessels] Blood supply, lymphatics and innervation
Supply | Detail
Superior laryngeal artery | A branch of the superior thyroid artery. Supplies the supraglottic part and anastomoses with the posteroinferior laryngeal artery
Anteroinferior laryngeal artery | A branch of the superior thyroid artery. Supplies the anterior part of the infraglottic stage
Posteroinferior laryngeal artery | A branch of the inferior thyroid artery. Supplies the posterior part of the laryngeal mucosa
Veins | Satellite to the arteries. Superior and inferior laryngeal veins to the superior thyroid vein, then the thyro-linguo-facial trunk, then the internal jugular vein. Posterior laryngeal vein to the inferior thyroid vein, then the subclavian vein
Lymphatics | Very rich in the supraglottic zone, thinner in the subglottic zone, absent at the vocal cords. Supraglottic: the internal jugular chain. Infraglottic: pretracheal and paratracheal nodes and the internal jugular chain
Nerve | Supply
Superior laryngeal nerve | A branch of the vagus, mixed but essentially sensory, descending against the pharyngeal wall and dividing behind the greater horn of the hyoid
Internal branch | Enters the larynx with the superior laryngeal artery by perforating the thyrohyoid membrane. Sensory innervation of the supraglottic mucosa, plus autonomic fibres
External branch | Innervates the cricothyroid muscle and perforates the cricothyroid membrane; sensory innervation of the subglottic stage
Recurrent (inferior) laryngeal nerve | From the vagus, on the right at the base of the neck and on the left at the thorax. Motor to all the intrinsic muscles except the cricothyroid; sensory below the vocal folds through an ascending branch anastomosing with the superior laryngeal nerve: Galen's loop

[sec:anat3-larynx/phonation] Physiology of phonation
The set of mechanisms allowing vibration at the free edge of the vocal fold, transforming aerodynamic energy into acoustic energy. The sound produced is immediately modified and filtered by the supralaryngeal air cavities to become vowels and consonants: the bucco-pharyngeal resonators.
The voice is characterised by three acoustic parameters: frequency, in Hertz, depending on the vibration speed of the vocal folds; intensity, in dB, depending on the force of pulmonary expiration (subglottic pressure); and pitch, depending on the accommodation of the vocal folds but especially on the shape of the supralaryngeal cavities.
Respiratory changes. During phonation the expiratory time lengthens and becomes active, the inspiratory time shortens, the mobilised air volumes are greater and expiratory lung pressures increase. The expiratory blow creates a subglottic pressure that overcomes the resistance of the vocal cords.
The vibratory cycle. 1. Putting into phonatory position: the folds close as the arytenoids come together. 2. Opening: when the subglottic pressure overcomes the muscular and elastic resistance, a puff of air passes at high speed, an acoustic impulse. 3. Closure of the folds.
The supralaryngeal cavities. Filter role: resonance and enrichment of certain frequencies or suppression of others, with two areas of particular enhancement, the pharynx and the oral cavity. The basic laryngeal frequency is enriched with harmonics giving the pitch. Articulation role: through the tongue, pharyngolarynx, jaws, lips and soft palate. Dry or cold ambient air can change the voice through mucous changes in the walls.
Mechanism, step by step. 1. The vocal cords are kept adducted. 2. Infraglottic air pressure is generated by exhaled air from the lungs. 3. The force of air opens the cords and is released as small puffs. 4. Moving air passing through the cords makes them vibrate, producing sound. 5. Sound is amplified by the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus and nose. 6. Sound is converted into speech by the lips, tongue, palate, pharynx and teeth.
Vowels are voiced in the larynx by vibration of the vocal folds; consonants are produced by the intrinsic muscles of the tongue. Loudness depends on the amplitude of vibration, pitch on the frequency. Male cords are usually longer, so they vibrate with greater amplitude but lower frequency: louder but lower pitched.

[sec:anat3-larynx/clinical] Clinical correlations
Safety muscles of the larynx. The posterior cricoarytenoids are the only intrinsic muscles that abduct the vocal cords, letting air through the rima glottidis. When they contract, the muscular processes of both arytenoids rotate medially and the vocal processes rotate laterally, giving a wide diamond-shaped opening. If they are paralysed, the adductors take the upper hand and the person might die from lack of air.
Tuning fork of the larynx. The cricothyroid is important for the tone and pitch of the voice: when sound is about to be produced it tenses the cord and makes it ready to vibrate. Paralysis after an external laryngeal nerve lesion alters the voice significantly, especially noticeable in singers.
Nerve damage. External laryngeal nerve: weakness of phonation from loss of the tightening effect of the cricothyroid. Recurrent laryngeal nerve, often damaged accidentally during partial thyroidectomy: unilaterally, the cord lies in the paramedian position and does not vibrate, though the other usually compensates; bilaterally, both cords lie paramedian with loss of phonation and difficulty breathing. If both recurrent and external laryngeal nerves are damaged on both sides, all the intrinsic muscles are paralysed and the cords are further abducted and fixed: the cadaveric position.
Laryngeal obstruction. The mucosa of the superior larynx is very sensitive, so a foreign body causes immediate explosive coughing. If that reflex is slowed or absent, choking follows. The Heimlich manoeuvre: stand behind the victim, pass your arms under theirs, place your hands in front of the epigastrium with one hand as a fist and the other over it, then give three or four abdominal thrusts directed upwards and backwards, squeezing the residual air up to dislodge the foreign body.
Vocal nodules (singer's or screamer's nodules). The area of maximum contact during vibration is at the junction of the anterior one third and posterior two thirds of the cords, so it takes the most friction. People who overuse the voice develop inflammatory nodules there: bilateral, symmetrical, from pin head to split pea in size, soft, reddish and oedematous early, later greyish or whitish.

[deeper:1] Why lymphatics explain laryngeal cancer staging
The lecture's line that lymphatics are absent at the vocal cords has a large consequence. A tumour confined to the cord spreads late and has a good outlook, while a supraglottic tumour, in a very richly drained zone, reaches the internal jugular nodes early. That is why the three compartments of the larynx are staged separately, and why hoarseness in a smoker is investigated urgently.
Source: the lecture's lymphatic drainage slide and its topographical compartments.

[deeper:2] Why the left recurrent nerve is the longer one
The lecture notes that the recurrent nerve arises on the right at the base of the neck and on the left in the thorax. That is embryology: the nerve hooks under the sixth aortic arch, which on the right regresses so the nerve rides up on the subclavian artery, while on the left it stays as the ligamentum arteriosum, dragging the nerve down under the aortic arch. The long left course is why a lung or mediastinal mass can cause hoarseness.
Source: the lecture's description of the recurrent nerve origins.

[deeper:3] Why the cricothyroid membrane is the emergency airway
The median cricothyroid ligament sits just under the skin between two easily felt landmarks, the thyroid cartilage above and the cricoid below, with no major vessels crossing it and no thyroid gland in the way. That is why an emergency airway is made there rather than through the trachea lower down, where the thyroid isthmus and vessels lie.
Source: the lecture's extrinsic ligaments and relations slides.

[deeper:4] Why children choke more dangerously
The lecture flags the glottis as a narrow zone in children, where obstructive oedema causes respiratory distress very rapidly. Airway resistance rises with the fourth power of the radius, so a millimetre of swelling in a small larynx cuts the airway far more than the same swelling in an adult. It is why croup and epiglottitis are emergencies in small children.

Front: Define the larynx.
Back: The upper expanded part of the lower respiratory tract, modified for producing voice, also called the organ of phonation. An unpaired median tube of fibro-muscular-cartilaginous constitution: several cartilages linked by joints, muscles and ligaments, lined on their deep surface by a mucous membrane.

Back: Phonation, respiration, protection and deglutition. It acts as a sphincter at the inlet of the lower respiratory tract to protect the trachea and bronchial tree from anything other than air. Laryngeal incompetence means food and fluid may be aspirated. Its upward and downward movements also help in swallowing.

Back: In the middle part of the visceral sheath of the neck. Above: the hyoid bone and base of the tongue. Below: the trachea. Behind: the hypopharynx. In front: the thyroid. It is higher in children (C2 to C3) than in adults; in newborns it contacts the veil, which is why they breathe exclusively through the nose until about four months.

[card:anat3-larynx/l5]
Front: Give the extension, shape and dimensions of the larynx.
Back: Superior: the upper border of the thyroid cartilage at the body of C4. Inferior: the lower edge of the cricoid at the lower edge of C6. A triangular pyramid with a posterosuperior base (hyoid bone, pharynx) and a lower vertex (trachea). In men, length 4.5 cm, transverse diameter 4 cm, sagittal diameter 3.5 cm; smaller in women.

[card:anat3-larynx/g1]
Front: Which cartilage is at the arrow?
Back: The thyroid cartilage, shaped like an open book with two quadrilateral laminae. Its superior border carries the superior thyroid notch.

[card:anat3-larynx/l6]
Front: How is the larynx fixed in place?
Back: It is mobile in the vertical and transverse directions but held by its continuity with the pharynx and trachea, and by its union with the hyoid bone and the thoracic skeleton through muscles and ligaments.

[card:anat3-larynx/l7]
Front: Name the nine laryngeal cartilages.
Back: Unpaired and large: thyroid, cricoid, epiglottis. Paired and small: arytenoid, corniculate, cuneiform. The principal cartilages are the cricoid, the thyroid and the two arytenoids.

[card:anat3-larynx/l8]
Front: Describe the thyroid cartilage.
Back: Shaped like an open book, open backwards, protecting the elements of the larynx, with two vertical quadrilateral laminae. Each has an anterolateral side, a posteromedial side (smooth, related to the piriform sinus), a superior border with the superior thyroid incisure, an inferior border with the inferior thyroid incisure, and a vertical thick posterior margin extended by the superior and inferior cornua, the inferior one carrying a cricoid articular surface.

[card:anat3-larynx/l9]
Front: Describe the epiglottis.
Back: Shaped like a tennis racket with a curved lower handle forming its foot. Laryngeal or posterior side: respiratory-type mucosa with numerous cribriform depressions, facing the laryngeal vestibule. Lingual or anterior side: concave, facing the base of the tongue and the hyoid bone. Base: superior and free. Vertex or foot: inferior, inserting into the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage through the thyroepiglottic ligament.

[card:anat3-larynx/l10]
Front: Describe the cricoid cartilage.
Back: A signet-shaped ring with a narrow anterior arch and a broad posterior lamina, at the level of C6. It completely encircles the lumen of the larynx and is the foundation stone of the larynx. The posterior surface of the lamina has a median ridge with a depressed area on each side.

[card:anat3-larynx/g2]
Front: Which cartilage is at the arrow, and what is unique about it?
Back: The cricoid cartilage, at C6: the only complete ring of cartilage in the airway, signet-shaped with a narrow anterior arch and a broad posterior lamina. The foundation stone of the larynx.

[card:anat3-larynx/l11]
Front: Describe the arytenoid cartilages.
Back: Right and left, shaped like triangular pyramids, arranged vertically, about 15 mm high. The apex articulates with the corniculate cartilage and the concave base with the articular facet of the cricoid lamina. The anterior angle of the base is extended by the vocal process, giving insertion to the vocal ligament. The lateral angle bears the muscular process, where the posterior and lateral cricoarytenoid muscles insert. The posterior side gives insertion to the transverse arytenoid muscle.

[card:anat3-larynx/l12]
Front: Name the accessory cartilages.
Back: Corniculate: rest on the apices of the arytenoids. Cuneiform: in the aryepiglottic fold. Others: posterior and anterior sesamoids and interarytenoid.

[card:anat3-larynx/l13]
Front: Describe the cricoarytenoid joint and its movements.
Back: The most important functionally: an elliptical synovial articulation reinforced by the posterior cricoarytenoid ligament, uniting the base of the arytenoid with the upper edge of the cricoid lamina. 1. Rotatory movement: the arytenoid moves around a vertical axis, abducting or adducting the vocal cords. 2. Gliding movement: one arytenoid glides toward or away from the other, closing or opening the posterior part of the glottis.

[card:anat3-larynx/l14]
Front: Describe the cricothyroid joint.
Back: A flat synovial joint reinforced by ligaments, joining the lower cornu of the thyroid cartilage and the lateral face of the cricoid. Movement: tilting of the cricoid around a transverse axis, the arch up and the lamina down, tensing the vocal cords.

[card:anat3-larynx/l15]
Front: Name the extrinsic ligaments and membranes.
Back: The thyrohyoid membrane, from the upper edge of the thyroid cartilage to the lower edge of the hyoid, with a medial thickening (medial thyroid ligament) and two lateral thickenings (lateral thyroid ligaments). The thyroepiglottic ligament, attaching the lower narrow end of the epiglottis to the posterior surface of the thyroid angle. The hyoepiglottic ligament, connecting the posterior aspect of the hyoid with the anterior surface of the upper epiglottis. The cricotracheal ligament, between the lower edge of the cricoid and the upper edge of the first tracheal ring.

[card:anat3-larynx/g3]
Front: Which cartilage is at the arrow?
Back: The epiglottis, shaped like a tennis racket. Its foot inserts into the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage through the thyroepiglottic ligament.

[card:anat3-larynx/l16]
Front: Name the intrinsic ligaments.
Back: They are parts of a broad sheet of fibroelastic tissue forming the inner tube of the laryngeal cavity outside its mucous lining. The cricothyroid ligament, stretched between the thyroid cartilage and the vocal process, forms the vocal ligament. The vestibular ligament is tense between the thyroid angle and the anterolateral aspect of the arytenoid.

[card:anat3-larynx/l17]
Front: Classify the intrinsic muscles by action.
Back: 1. Vocal cord tensors: cricothyroid (paired), elevating the cricoid arch and lowering the thyroid cartilage. 2. Dilator of the glottis: the posterior cricoarytenoid, the only one. 3. Constrictors of the glottis: lateral cricoarytenoid, interarytenoid, superior thyroarytenoid and inferior thyroarytenoid (the vocal muscle), which tilts the arytenoid forward, tensing the cord.

[card:anat3-larynx/l18]
Front: Which muscles abduct and which adduct the vocal cords?
Back: Abduct: the posterior cricoarytenoids. Adduct: the lateral cricoarytenoids and the transverse arytenoid.

[card:anat3-larynx/l19]
Front: Which muscles change the tension of the vocal cords?
Back: Tense: the cricothyroid and the vocalis. Relax: the thyroarytenoid.

[card:anat3-larynx/l20]
Front: Give the three exceptions worth remembering about the intrinsic muscles.
Back: The cricothyroid is the only muscle lying on the outer aspect of the larynx. All the intrinsic muscles are paired except the transverse arytenoid (interarytenoid), which is unpaired. All of them adduct the vocal cords except the posterior cricoarytenoids, which abduct them.

[card:anat3-larynx/g4]
Front: Which bone is at the arrow?
Back: The hyoid bone. It is closely associated with the larynx but is usually considered a separate structure.

[card:anat3-larynx/l21]
Front: Why are the posterior cricoarytenoids called the safety muscles?
Back: They are the only intrinsic muscles that abduct the vocal cords, allowing air through the rima glottidis. When they contract, the muscular processes of both arytenoids rotate medially so the vocal processes rotate laterally, giving a wide diamond-shaped opening. If they are paralysed the adductors take the upper hand and the person may die from lack of air.

[card:anat3-larynx/l22]
Front: Name the extrinsic muscles of the larynx.
Back: They ensure laryngeal mobility, especially during swallowing. Elevators: thyrohyoid, mylohyoid and digastric. Depressors: sternothyroid, sternohyoid and omohyoid.

[card:anat3-larynx/l23]
Front: Describe the supraglottic compartment.
Back: Vestibule: a pyramidal space extending from the laryngeal margin to the ventricular bands. Laryngeal or Morgagni ventricle: the cavity between the ventricular bands (false vocal cords) above and the plane of the vocal cords below. Interarytenoid region: the posterior part of the supraglottic stage, where the mucous membrane covers the fibres of the interarytenoid muscle.

[card:anat3-larynx/l24]
Front: Describe the glottis.
Back: Two pearly white vocal cords stretched between the reentrant angle of the thyroid cartilage in front and the arytenoid cartilage behind, made of vocal ligament and muscle. Anteriorly they unite at the anterior commissure, attached directly to the thyroid cartilage and containing vessels and lymphatics. Posteriorly they are separated by the interarytenoid region, the posterior commissure. The glottis is triangular and narrower than the vestibular cleft. It is a narrow zone in children, so obstructive oedema causes respiratory distress very rapidly.

[card:anat3-larynx/l25]
Front: Describe the infraglottic compartment.
Back: The cavity below the vocal cords, surrounded by the cricoid and the cricothyroid ligament.

[card:anat3-larynx/g5]
Front: Which membrane is at the arrow?
Back: The thyrohyoid membrane, from the upper edge of the thyroid cartilage to the lower edge of the hyoid. The internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve pierces it with the superior laryngeal artery.

[card:anat3-larynx/l26]
Front: Give the histology of the larynx.
Back: The laryngeal mucosa is thin, smooth and pale pink, continuous above with the pharyngeal mucosa and below with the tracheal mucosa. Epithelium: respiratory type (cylindric stratified) throughout, except at the vocal cords and the upper half of the epiglottis, which are non-keratinized stratified squamous.

[card:anat3-larynx/l27]
Front: Name the relations of the larynx.
Back: Above: the base of the tongue. Below: the trachea. In front: the musculoaponeurotic planes of the subhyoid region and the covering planes, where the larynx is very superficial. Behind: the hypopharynx and cervical spine. Laterally: the piriform sinuses medial to the thyroid cartilage, then the thyroid lobes, and further out the elements of the carotid region.

[card:anat3-larynx/l28]
Front: Give the arterial supply of the larynx.
Back: Superior laryngeal artery, a branch of the superior thyroid artery, supplying the supraglottic part and anastomosing with the posteroinferior laryngeal artery. Anteroinferior laryngeal artery, also from the superior thyroid artery, supplying the anterior infraglottic stage. Posteroinferior laryngeal artery, from the inferior thyroid artery, supplying the posterior laryngeal mucosa.

[card:anat3-larynx/l29]
Front: Give the venous and lymphatic drainage.
Back: Veins are satellite to the arteries: the superior and inferior laryngeal veins drain to the superior thyroid vein, then the thyro-linguo-facial trunk, then the internal jugular vein; the posterior laryngeal vein drains to the inferior thyroid vein and the subclavian vein. Lymphatics are very rich in the supraglottic zone, thinner in the subglottic zone and absent at the vocal cords. Supraglottic: the internal jugular chain. Infraglottic: pretracheal and paratracheal nodes and the internal jugular chain.

[card:anat3-larynx/l30]
Front: Describe the superior laryngeal nerve.
Back: A branch of the vagus, mixed but essentially sensory, descending against the pharyngeal wall. It divides behind the greater horn of the hyoid into two branches. Internal: enters the larynx with the superior laryngeal artery by perforating the thyrohyoid membrane, giving sensory innervation to the supraglottic mucosa. External: innervates the cricothyroid muscle and perforates the cricothyroid membrane, giving sensory innervation of the subglottic stage.

[card:anat3-larynx/g6]
Front: Which structure is at the arrow?
Back: The median cricothyroid ligament, between the cricoid and thyroid cartilages. Its intrinsic part forms the vocal ligament.

[card:anat3-larynx/l31]
Front: Describe the recurrent (inferior) laryngeal nerve.
Back: It originates from the vagus, on the right at the base of the neck and on the left at the level of the thorax. It innervates all the intrinsic muscles except the cricothyroid, and gives sensory innervation of the subglottis through an ascending branch that anastomoses with the superior laryngeal nerve: Galen's loop.

[card:anat3-larynx/l32]
Front: What happens when the recurrent laryngeal nerve is damaged?
Back: It is often damaged accidentally during partial thyroidectomy. Unilateral: the cord on the affected side lies in the paramedian position, between abduction and adduction, and does not vibrate; usually the other cord compensates and phonation is not much affected. Bilateral: both cords lie in the paramedian position, with loss of phonation and difficulty breathing. If the recurrent and external laryngeal nerves are damaged on both sides, all intrinsic muscles are paralysed and the cords are further abducted and fixed: the cadaveric position.

[card:anat3-larynx/l33]
Front: What is laryngeal obstruction, and how is it relieved?
Back: The mucous membrane of the superior larynx is very sensitive: a foreign body entering the laryngeal inlet causes immediate explosive coughing. If that reflex is slowed or absent, as in a neurological lesion or after alcohol, a foreign body may enter and cause choking. The Heimlich manoeuvre relieves it: stand behind the victim, pass your arms under theirs, place your hands in front of the epigastrium with one hand as a fist and the other over it, then give three or four abdominal thrusts directed upwards and backwards.

[card:anat3-larynx/l34]
Front: What are vocal nodules?
Back: Singer's or screamer's nodules. During vibration the area of maximum contact between the cords is at the junction of their anterior one third and posterior two thirds, which takes the most friction. People who overuse the voice, such as teachers and pop singers, develop inflammatory nodules there. They are bilateral and symmetrical, varying from pin head to split pea size, soft, reddish and oedematous early, later greyish or whitish.

[card:anat3-larynx/l35]
Front: Define phonation.
Back: The set of mechanisms allowing vibration at the free edge of the vocal fold, transforming aerodynamic energy into acoustic energy. The sound produced is immediately modified and filtered by the supralaryngeal air cavities to become vowels and consonants: the bucco-pharyngeal resonators.

[card:anat3-larynx/g7]
Front: Which landmark is at the arrow?
Back: The laryngeal prominence, the Adam's apple, formed where the two laminae of the thyroid cartilage meet in the midline.

[card:anat3-larynx/l36]
Front: Give the three acoustic parameters of the voice.
Back: Frequency, in Hertz, depending on the vibration speed of the vocal folds (opening and closing cycles per second). Intensity, in dB, depending on the force of pulmonary expiration (subglottic pressure). Pitch, depending on the accommodation of the vocal folds but especially on the shape of the supralaryngeal cavities.

[card:anat3-larynx/l37]
Front: Describe the vibratory cycle.
Back: 1. Putting into phonatory position: the vocal folds close as the arytenoids come together. 2. Opening: when the subglottic pressure is sufficient to overcome the muscular and elastic forces of resistance, a puff of air passes at high speed, an acoustic impulse. 3. Closure of the vocal folds.

[card:anat3-larynx/l38]
Front: What do the supralaryngeal cavities do?
Back: A filter role: resonance and enrichment of certain frequencies and suppression of others, with two areas of particular enhancement, the pharynx and the oral cavity; the basic laryngeal frequency is enriched with harmonics giving the pitch. An articulation role: under the effect of the articulatory muscles, the tongue, pharyngolarynx, jaws, lips and soft palate. Dry or cold ambient air can change the voice through mucous changes in the walls.

[card:anat3-larynx/l39]
Front: Why is a man's voice louder but lower pitched?
Back: Loudness depends on the amplitude of the vibrating vocal folds, pitch on their frequency. The vocal cords are usually longer in males than females, so they vibrate with greater amplitude but lower frequency.

[card:anat3-larynx/g8]
Front: Which structure is at the arrow, and what connects it to the larynx?
Back: The trachea. The cricotracheal ligament runs between the lower edge of the cricoid and the upper edge of the first tracheal ring.
